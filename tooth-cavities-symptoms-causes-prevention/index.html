Tooth Cavities: Symptoms, Causes and Prevention

By Footscray Dental Studio Team · 20 April 2026 · Updated 16 September 2026

Tooth cavities are one of the most common health problems in Australia, with around one in three adults living with untreated tooth decay. The encouraging news is that cavities are largely preventable, and when they are caught early they are usually straightforward to treat. In this guide, the team at Footscray Dental Studio explains what a cavity actually is, how it develops, the symptoms to watch for, and the practical steps that help adults and children keep decay at bay.

What Is a Tooth Cavity?

A dental cavity is a damaged area on the outer surface of a tooth that gradually develops into tiny holes or openings. It rarely appears overnight. Instead, decay chips away at the tooth over weeks and months, and by the time you notice sharp pain, persistent bad breath or staining on the tooth, the damage is often well established.

Cavities do not discriminate by age. Babies, children, adults and older adults can all develop them, and the extent of the damage depends on where the tooth sits in the mouth and how consistently it has been cared for. Molars are particularly prone to cavities because their grooved chewing surfaces trap food particles, while front teeth, which are smoother and easier to clean, tend to be less susceptible. Neglect is the other big variable: the longer plaque and food debris are left undisturbed on a tooth, the further the damage spreads.

It also helps to understand the relationship between decay and cavities. Decay is the ongoing process of damage, while a cavity is the physical hole that process eventually creates. You can read more about the process itself in our companion article on tooth decay symptoms, causes and treatment methods.

How Does a Cavity Occur?

Cavities form through a predictable four-phase process: plaque formation, acid production, enamel demineralisation and finally cavity formation. Understanding each phase makes it much easier to see where good habits can interrupt the cycle.

1. Plaque Formation

Your mouth naturally contains bacteria. When those bacteria are fed a steady supply of high-sugar foods and drinks, they multiply and create a colourless, sticky layer on the teeth known as plaque. Plaque clings to the tooth surface and along the gumline, and if it is not brushed away it becomes the launching pad for decay.

2. Acid Production

The bacteria living in plaque react with the sugars in the food you eat, producing acids as a by-product. These acids sit against the tooth surface and attack the minerals in the enamel, the hard protective outer layer of each tooth.

3. Enamel Demineralisation

With repeated acid attacks, the enamel begins to lose minerals and becomes weaker and more vulnerable. During this phase you may notice chalky white spots appearing on the tooth surface. This is the earliest visible warning sign of decay, and at this stage the damage can often still be slowed or reversed with professional care and better daily habits.

4. Cavity Formation

If demineralisation continues unchecked, the weakened enamel eventually breaks down and tiny holes form, exposing the softer dentine underneath. From there, decay can spread deeper towards the pulp at the centre of the tooth, where it interferes with the blood vessels and nerves and causes pain and infection.

Common Symptoms of an Early-Stage Cavity

Cavities develop gradually, and the earliest signs often go unnoticed. As decay progresses, most people experience one or more of the following symptoms.

1. Tooth Sensitivity

A sharp ache or twinge of discomfort when you have hot, cold or sweet food and drinks is one of the most common early signs. Sensitivity happens because the weakened enamel is no longer insulating the nerve-rich dentine underneath. If your morning coffee or a cold drink on a warm Footscray afternoon suddenly makes you wince, it is worth having the tooth checked.

2. Toothache

Continuous pain or discomfort, particularly while chewing hard food, suggests the decay has moved beyond the surface. Toothache should never be ignored, because it usually means the deeper layers of the tooth are involved.

3. Persistent Bad Breath

Bacteria multiplying inside a decayed area of a tooth release unpleasant odours that brushing alone cannot fix. If bad breath keeps returning despite good hygiene, decay may be the culprit. We explain this in more detail in our guide to what causes a tooth decay smell.

4. Visible Marks and Stains

Black, brown or yellow stains on the front teeth or molars can indicate that decay is breaking through the enamel. Any new dark spot on a tooth deserves a professional look.

Major Causes of Cavities in Children and Adults

At its heart, dental decay stems from a combination of everyday lifestyle habits and gaps in oral hygiene. The main culprits are worth understanding, because each one is something you can act on.

Plaque Buildup and Poor Brushing Technique

Inadequate or improper brushing is one of the most common causes of cavities. Many people brush quickly and miss the molars and back teeth, where the brush is hardest to reach. Food particles accumulate in these neglected areas, bacteria build up and form plaque, and over time that plaque erodes the teeth, irritates the gums and leads to decay.

High Sugar Intake and Frequent Snacking

Chocolate bars, biscuits, lollies and soft drinks are everywhere, and enjoyed in moderation they are generally safe. The trouble starts when processed, sugary foods become a habit. Frequent snacking exposes the teeth to repeated acid attacks and leaves very little time for the enamel to remineralise between them. It also triggers ongoing plaque buildup, which can spiral into decay if it is not treated.

Dry Mouth and Lack of Fluoride

These two factors are often overlooked. Saliva is your mouth's natural defence system: it washes away food particles and neutralises acids, preventing plaque from taking hold. Prolonged dry mouth leaves a sticky environment with little saliva, which is ideal ground for decay. A shortage of fluoride has a similar effect from a different direction. Fluoride strengthens enamel and helps it resist acid attacks, so using a toothpaste with less fluoride than the recommended level quietly undermines that protection and can trigger dental decay.

Genetic Factors and Enamel Weakness

Some people are simply born with naturally thinner or weaker enamel, which makes their teeth more susceptible to acid erosion and bacterial attack. Certain inherited conditions also increase cavity risk even when oral hygiene is good. If dental problems run in your family, your teeth deserve extra care and more frequent professional attention.

Why Are Children More Vulnerable to Tooth Decay?

Children are especially prone to cavities in their molars, and the reasons are a mix of biology, behaviour and diet. A child's tooth enamel has lower resistance to bacterial and acid attacks than adult enamel, which makes it easier for plaque to gain a foothold. Their dentine structure is also weaker, and when that is combined with inconsistent or imperfect brushing habits, bacterial buildup develops quickly and contributes to decay.

Diet plays a big part too. Children tend to consume chocolates, lollies, juices and processed foods more freely than adults, and overconsumption causes food particles to stick around the teeth and work their way into the dentine, leading to decay. Add the fact that many children rush their brushing, or skip it altogether when no one is watching, and it is easy to see why childhood cavities are so common.

The practical answer for parents is supervision, sensible snack choices and early, regular dental visits so problems are found while they are still tiny. Eligible children may also receive bulk-billed care under the Child Dental Benefits Schedule, so cost should never be a reason to delay a child's check-up.

How Do Dentists Diagnose a Cavity?

Diagnosing a cavity properly requires a visit to the clinic and a consultation with a dentist, who follows a thorough clinical approach.

1. Visual Examination

The dentist examines each tooth carefully, either with the naked eye or with a dental mirror, looking for the early signs of decay such as white spots, staining and surface breakdown.

2. X-Ray Examination

Some cavities hide where no visual inspection can find them. Digital X-rays allow the dentist to detect decay between the teeth or beneath the enamel surface, long before it would become visible or painful. A routine check-up and clean at Footscray Dental Studio includes this kind of careful assessment.

It is worth noting that a medically safe and precise diagnosis of tooth decay can only be made by a registered dentist following clinical procedures in line with AHPRA guidelines. Online symptom checkers and guesswork are no substitute for a proper examination.

Treatment Options for Cavities

The right treatment depends on how far the decay has progressed. Suitability always depends on an individual assessment, and your dentist will talk you through the options. For a deeper look at each stage of treatment, see our guide on how to fix rotten teeth.

1. Dental Fillings

For most cavities, a dental filling is the recommended and effective option. The dentist removes the decayed part of the tooth, cleans the area thoroughly, then seals it with a durable filling material. This restores the tooth's shape, its strength and its function, including comfortable chewing, and the sealed surface guards against future cavities. Fillings do rely on ongoing oral hygiene to stay intact. Without proper care they can wear or chip over time, and a damaged filling will need to be replaced.

2. Root Canal Therapy

When early decay is ignored and advances beyond the enamel and dentine into the tooth's pulp, which contains the nerves and blood vessels, deep decay causes severe pain and infection and can even lead to a cyst forming. Root canal therapy addresses this by removing the infected pulp tissue, cleaning and disinfecting the inner canals, and sealing the cleaned space with a biocompatible filling material called gutta-percha. A dental crown is then usually fitted over the treated tooth to strengthen it and restore normal function.

3. Tooth Extraction

Extraction is the last resort, reserved for cases where decay has so severely damaged the tooth structure that a filling, crown or root canal loses its efficacy and can no longer save the tooth. Dentists recommend removal in these cases to stop the infection spreading to surrounding teeth, gums and bone. The procedure is performed under local anaesthetic to keep the patient comfortable, and aftercare guidance is provided to support healing and reduce the risk of infection. After an extraction, replacement options such as dental implants, dentures or bridges can be discussed, depending on the location and function of the tooth that was removed.

Cost of Treating Tooth Cavities in Australia

Costs vary from clinic to clinic and case to case, depending on the size of the cavity, the tooth involved and the materials used. The following are typical Australian market ranges, offered here as a general guide only rather than a quote:

  • Dental fillings (small cavity): $150 to $300
  • Dental fillings (large or complex cavity): $300 to $500
  • Root canal therapy (excluding the crown): $900 to $3,400
  • Dental crown after root canal (additional): $1,200 to $2,500
  • Simple tooth extraction: $150 to $400 per tooth
  • Complex or surgical extraction: $250 to $600 or more

The pattern is clear: the earlier decay is treated, the simpler and less expensive the treatment tends to be. A small filling costs a fraction of a root canal and crown, and preventive care costs less again. If you have private health insurance with extras cover, preventive and restorative dental work may attract a rebate, so it is worth checking your policy before you book.

Tips to Prevent Dental Decay in Adults and Children

Prevention is always better, cheaper and more comfortable than treatment. A few consistent habits make an enormous difference:

  • Brush daily with a fluoride toothpaste. Twice a day, for two minutes, covering every surface including those hard-to-reach back molars. Technique matters as much as frequency, so ask your dentist to check yours.
  • Keep up routine dental check-ups. Regular visits let your dentist catch demineralisation and early decay before a cavity ever forms, when the problem can still be managed without drilling.
  • Cut down on sugary foods and limit snacking. Reducing both the amount of sugar you eat and the frequency of snacking gives your enamel time to recover and remineralise between meals.
  • Ask about fluoride varnish and dental sealants. Professional fluoride treatments strengthen enamel against acid attack, and fissure sealants coat and protect the deep grooves of molars, which is especially valuable for children whose brushing is still a work in progress.

A small cavity is a small problem, but a neglected cavity rarely stays small. Serious cavity treatment calls for expert help, and that is exactly what our team provides.
