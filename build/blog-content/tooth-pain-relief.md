Few things disrupt an ordinary day quite like a toothache. Whether it is a dull ache that will not settle or a sharp jolt every time you sip something cold, tooth pain has a way of demanding attention. This guide from the team at Footscray Dental Studio walks through what causes tooth pain, which home measures genuinely help in the short term, what to avoid, and the warning signs that mean it is time to be seen by a dentist.

## Quick Overview

Tooth pain is any discomfort that comes from the teeth, gums, jaw or the surrounding structures of the mouth. It can range from mild sensitivity that flares with hot or cold drinks through to severe, throbbing pain that keeps you awake at night.

It is most commonly experienced by adults with untreated tooth decay, gum disease or emerging wisdom teeth, though it can affect anyone at any age. Temporary home measures such as saltwater rinses, cold compresses and over-the-counter pain relief can take the edge off while you arrange care, but they never treat the underlying cause. That distinction matters: a toothache that settles for an afternoon has not gone away, and whatever caused it is usually still progressing quietly in the background.

How quickly tooth pain progresses depends on what is driving it, how severe any infection is, and how promptly you seek care. If you develop a fever, notice facial swelling, or the pain lasts more than two days, prompt dental assessment matters. ## What Causes Tooth Pain?

Tooth pain happens when the nerve endings in or around a tooth become irritated or inflamed. Because several different problems can produce very similar pain, working out the cause usually needs a proper examination rather than guesswork. The most common culprits are decay, injury, wisdom teeth, abscesses and gum disease, but grinding, sinus trouble and cracked teeth all make regular appearances too. Here is how each one tends to behave.

![Common symptoms that point to the cause of tooth pain](/assets/img/blog/symptoms-as-reason-for-pain.webp)

### Tooth decay

Decay is the most frequent cause of toothache. Bacteria in the mouth produce acids that erode the outer layers of the tooth and, left unchecked, work their way towards the pulp, where the nerve lives. Small cavities are often silent, which is why they can grow unnoticed. Deeper decay tends to cause sensitivity or a persistent ache, and once the nerve is involved the result is usually severe pain and infection. It is a widespread problem: according to the [Australian Institute of Health and Welfare](https://www.aihw.gov.au/reports/dental-oral-health/oral-health-and-dental-care-in-australia), about one in three Australians aged 15 and over have at least one tooth with untreated decay.

### Gum disease

Inflammation and infection of the gum tissue can cause pain, sensitivity and swelling around the teeth. Early gum disease often announces itself gently, with tender gums or a little bleeding when you brush, which makes it easy to dismiss. In its advanced stages, though, gum disease compromises the bone and ligaments that hold your teeth in place, which is why gum-related pain should never be brushed aside.

### Dental abscess

An abscess is a pocket of infection that forms in a tooth or in the gum beside it. It typically causes throbbing, severe pain and may bring facial swelling, fever and a bad taste in the mouth. Unlike some aches that come and go, an abscess will not resolve on its own; it needs professional treatment, and the sooner it is drained and treated, the better the outcome tends to be.

### Wisdom teeth

Many people in their late teens and twenties feel pain as wisdom teeth emerge, particularly when there is limited space in the jaw. Food and bacteria can become trapped around partially erupted wisdom teeth, leading to decay and gum infection in the area. [Better Health Channel](https://www.betterhealth.vic.gov.au/health/conditionsandtreatments/wisdom-teeth) has helpful general information on wisdom teeth and the problems they can cause.

### Cracked or fractured tooth

A crack can expose the nerve inside the tooth to temperature changes and chewing pressure. The pain is often sharp and intermittent, and it can be surprisingly hard to pinpoint which tooth is responsible.

### Teeth grinding (bruxism)

Sustained grinding or clenching puts significant pressure on the teeth and jaw. Over time this can cause tooth soreness, sensitivity and jaw pain, often worst first thing in the morning. If your lower teeth in particular ache when you wake, our post on [why your bottom teeth might hurt](/why-do-my-bottom-teeth-hurt/) explores this in more detail.

### Sinus infection

The roots of the upper back teeth sit close to the sinuses, so sinus pressure can mimic a toothache in the upper teeth. If your tooth pain arrived alongside nasal congestion and facial pressure, and especially if several upper teeth ache at once, a sinus infection is worth considering. Sinus-related tooth pain usually settles as the sinus problem clears, but a dental check helps rule out a genuine tooth issue hiding behind it.

## What Helps With Tooth Pain at Home?

The measures below can provide genuine short-term relief while you wait to see a dentist. They do not treat the cause, and individual responses vary, but used sensibly they can make the wait more bearable. It also helps to avoid the things that make toothache worse: very hot, very cold, sugary or acidic foods and drinks all tend to aggravate an irritated nerve, so keep meals soft and temperature-neutral until you are seen.

- **Saltwater rinse.** Dissolve half a teaspoon of salt in a cup of warm water, hold it in your mouth for up to two minutes, then spit it out. Do not swallow it. Salt water helps reduce bacteria and soothes irritated gum tissue.
- **Cold compress.** Hold a wrapped ice pack against your cheek for 15 to 20 minutes at a time. The cold helps reduce swelling and is particularly useful for pain following an injury.
- **Over-the-counter pain relief.** Paracetamol or ibuprofen, taken according to the directions on the packet, can help manage most toothache. Ibuprofen is a good option when there is swelling, because it also reduces inflammation. One important warning: never place aspirin directly on the gum next to a sore tooth. It burns the soft tissue and makes things worse.
- **Clove oil.** Clove oil contains eugenol, a natural compound with published evidence supporting its temporary numbing effect. Dilute it with a carrier oil, apply a small amount to a cotton ball, and hold it briefly against the sore area. Avoid swallowing it, and never use it undiluted, as it can irritate the tissue.
- **Keep your head elevated.** Lying flat increases blood pressure around the affected tooth, which is why toothache so often feels worse at night. Propping your head up with an extra pillow can ease the throbbing.
- **Keep the area clean.** Gently brush with a soft toothbrush and fluoride toothpaste after meals. A clean mouth gives bacteria less to feed on and can reduce irritation around the sore tooth. It is tempting to avoid the area entirely, but letting food debris sit against a painful tooth usually makes things worse.

A word of caution: you may come across online claims promising to switch off a tooth nerve instantly at home. These claims are misleading and some of the suggested methods are genuinely harmful. ## When Should You See a Dentist for Tooth Pain?

![Dentist examining a patient's mouth to find the source of tooth pain](/assets/img/blog/dentist-checking-a-patients-mouth.webp)

Home measures buy time; they do not replace treatment. Arrange a dental appointment if you notice any of the following:

- Pain that lasts more than two days
- Pain that is not improving with over-the-counter relief
- A fever above 38°C
- Swelling of the face or jaw
- A persistent bad taste in your mouth
- Pain radiating to your ear, jaw or neck
- Difficulty opening your mouth or swallowing

Treat it as an **emergency** if swelling is spreading towards your cheek, eye or neck, if you have any difficulty breathing or swallowing, or if severe pain is rapidly getting worse. In rare cases a dental infection can spread to the surrounding structures of the head and neck, which is a serious medical situation. A dental abscess never resolves by itself, so do not wait it out. When you come in, your dentist will examine the painful area, ask about how and when the pain behaves, and usually take an X-ray, since many causes of toothache (deep decay, cracks, root-tip infections) are invisible to the naked eye. Depending on what the examination reveals, treatment might be as simple as a filling, or, where the nerve is infected, [root canal therapy](/services/restorative/root-canal/) to remove the infection while preserving the natural tooth. In some situations gum treatment, repair of a crack, or management of grinding will be the answer instead. The point is that the fix follows the diagnosis, not the other way around.

Reliable general information on toothache is also available from [Healthdirect](https://www.healthdirect.gov.au/toothache-and-swelling) and the Australian Dental Association's [teeth.org.au](https://www.teeth.org.au/toothache).

## A Considered Approach to Tooth Pain

Pain is your body's signal that something needs attention, and toothache is no exception. Because the right treatment depends entirely on the cause, and because outcomes vary from person to person, there is no single fix that suits everyone. Suitability for any treatment always depends on an individual assessment.

As a general rule: if tooth pain lasts more than a day or two, or if any of the warning signs above appear, book an assessment. Acting early almost always means simpler, more conservative treatment than waiting until the problem forces your hand. A small cavity caught this month is a straightforward filling; the same cavity ignored for a year can mean an infected nerve and a much more involved course of treatment. The pattern holds across nearly every cause of toothache, which is why we would always rather see a patient a little too early than a little too late.

And if you have run into online claims promising instant fixes for nerve pain, treat them with caution; safe, effective relief starts with finding the cause.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can tooth pain be caused by something other than a dental problem?

Yes. Sinus infections commonly mimic toothache in the upper teeth, usually alongside congestion and facial pressure. Jaw joint (TMD) problems can radiate pain into the teeth and jaw, and in rare cases pain referred from the heart can be felt in the jaw. A dental assessment helps determine whether the origin is dental or something else.

### Why does my tooth hurt only when I bite down?

Pain that appears only on biting is often caused by a cracked tooth, a loose or failing filling, or an infection at the tip of the root. These problems can be hard to detect without an X-ray, so your dentist will usually combine bite tests with imaging to find the source. Left unassessed, bite-specific pain tends to worsen over time.
